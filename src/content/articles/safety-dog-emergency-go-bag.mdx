    <h1>How to build a dog emergency go-bag</h1>
    <p class="lede">
      Keep your dog chill during emergencies: what goes in a dog emergency go-bag, what to stage beside it, and the records to have ready if you need to evacuate.
    </p>

  <div class="prose">
    <p>
      An evacuation order rarely arrives at a convenient moment. A wildfire jumps a ridge, a flood watch becomes a warning, an earthquake damages the house. You may have minutes, and you will be gathering a dog who is reading your stress. Supplies gathered in advance take most of the decisions out of those minutes.
    </p>
    <p>
      The goal is a setup you can grab in one trip: a single bag of immediate essentials, with the larger gear waiting right beside it. It earns its keep in smaller disruptions too — a car breakdown, getting stranded while traveling, an unexpected night away, or a sudden family or veterinary emergency.
    </p>

    <h2 id="bag-and-reserve">The go-bag and the staged reserve</h2>
    <p>
      A dog emergency go-bag is <strong>one portable bag holding the immediate essentials</strong>. It is not meant to contain every emergency supply your dog might need — a crate, a bed, and two weeks of food do not fit in a backpack. Split the setup in two:
    </p>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>Portable layer (the bag).</strong> Roughly 3 days of immediately usable food, water, and medication, plus ID, records, leash, and basic gear — as much as weight and space make sensible.</li>
      <li><strong>Staged reserve (beside the bag).</strong> The crate or carrier, bedding, and enough additional food, water, and medication for a longer displacement.</li>
    </ul>
    <p>
      The 3-day figure is our packing rule of thumb for the bag, not an official standard. Authoritative guidance is more conservative. The <a href="https://www.cdc.gov/healthy-pets/emergency-preparedness/preparedness-kit.html" rel="noopener noreferrer">CDC's pet disaster kit guidance</a> calls for a 2-week supply of food, water, and any medications. The <a href="https://www.aspca.org/news/disaster-preparedness-pets-what-plan-and-pack" rel="noopener noreferrer">ASPCA's September 2026 preparedness guide</a> calls for at least 7–10 days of food and water and 2 weeks of medication.
    </p>
    <p>
      The layers solve different problems. The bag covers the first days, when you may be on foot or in a shelter line. The reserve covers the longer stretch when you cannot get home and your dog's usual food is not on the shelf. Keep the physical bag portable, but don't mistake the contents of the bag for your entire emergency reserve.
    </p>

    <h3>Choosing the bag</h3>
    <p>
      You do not need specialized survival luggage; a sturdy backpack or duffel you already own is fine. Look for one that is easy to carry (ideally hands-free, since you will be holding a leash), weather-resistant, compartmentalized enough to separate documents, food, medication, and gear, and recognizable at a glance. The <a href="https://www.redcross.org/get-help/how-to-prepare-for-emergencies/pet-disaster-preparedness.html" rel="noopener noreferrer">American Red Cross</a> advises keeping supplies somewhere accessible, in sturdy containers that can be carried easily. A multi-pocket dog travel backpack is one option if you want the compartments built in.
    </p>

    <h2 id="what-goes-in-the-bag">What goes in a dog emergency go-bag</h2>
    <p>
      Pack the true essentials first, then stop:
    </p>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>Food</strong> — about 3 days of your dog's regular food, pre-portioned and sealed</li>
      <li><strong>Drinking water</strong> for the portable layer</li>
      <li><strong>Collapsible or non-spill bowls</strong></li>
      <li><strong>Required medications</strong>, labeled, with instructions</li>
      <li><strong>Spare leash</strong> and a <strong>secure collar and/or harness</strong></li>
      <li><strong>Backup slip lead</strong> — fast on a dog that slipped its harness</li>
      <li><strong>Current identification</strong>, including a spare tagged collar</li>
      <li><strong>Compact canine first-aid kit</strong></li>
      <li><strong>Waste bags and paper towels</strong></li>
      <li><strong>Flashlight or headlamp</strong>, with spare batteries if needed</li>
      <li><strong>Power bank and charging cable</strong> — your phone holds maps, contacts, and records</li>
      <li><strong>Documents packet</strong> (below)</li>
    </ul>
    <p>
      If the bag already feels heavy, trim quantities rather than adding a second bag — that is what the reserve is for. Several items overlap with our <a href={ROUTES.dogSnakeBiteEmergencyKit} data-track="collector_to_converter_click" data-from-page={pageSlug} data-to-page={ROUTES.dogSnakeBiteEmergencyKit} data-link-position="what-goes-in-the-bag">dog snake-bite emergency kit</a> — slip leads, headlamps, power banks — so there is no need to buy those twice.
    </p>

    <h2 id="identification">Identification and preventing separation</h2>
    <p>
      Evacuations create every condition for separation: unfamiliar places, doors opening constantly, crowded shelters and hotel lobbies, noise, and a stressed dog that may bolt at the first siren. Layer identification so losing one layer does not mean losing your dog:
    </p>
    <ol class="action-list">
      <li><strong>A current ID tag.</strong> The <a href="https://www.aspca.org/news/disaster-preparedness-pets-what-plan-and-pack" rel="noopener noreferrer">ASPCA</a> recommends tags with your dog's name, your phone number, and any urgent medical needs.</li>
      <li><strong>A registered microchip with current contact information.</strong> A chip only works if the registry record is current. <a href="https://www.ready.gov/pets" rel="noopener noreferrer">Ready.gov</a> recommends keeping your address and phone number up to date and adding an emergency contact outside your immediate area.</li>
      <li><strong>Optional: a GPS tracker.</strong> Useful, not essential — it depends on battery and cell coverage, both scarce in a disaster. Our <a href={ROUTES.trackingTop} data-track="collector_to_converter_click" data-from-page={pageSlug} data-to-page={ROUTES.trackingTop} data-link-position="identification">GPS tracker comparison</a> covers the trade-offs.</li>
    </ol>
    <div class="geo-callout">
      <p><strong>More than one dog?</strong> Scale everything per dog — quantities, ID, restraints, medication, and records — and confirm every dog and crate fits in the vehicle you would leave in.</p>
    </div>

    <h2 id="documents">Documents and emergency information</h2>
    <p>
      Records are the part of the kit most people skip, and the part that matters most at a shelter, boarding facility, or unfamiliar vet. The <a href="https://www.redcross.org/get-help/how-to-prepare-for-emergencies/pet-disaster-preparedness.html" rel="noopener noreferrer">Red Cross</a> notes that many pet shelters require proof of current vaccinations. If you are separated, documents are how you prove the dog is yours.
    </p>
    <p>
      Keep <strong>one slim waterproof packet per dog</strong>, topped by a one-page emergency summary that a shelter volunteer or friend can read in a minute:
    </p>
    <ul>
      <li>Dog's name, description, and microchip number</li>
      <li>Your phone number and a backup contact method</li>
      <li>Veterinarian and an out-of-area emergency contact</li>
      <li>Medications and how they are given</li>
      <li>Feeding instructions</li>
      <li>Medical conditions, allergies, behavior notes</li>
      <li>Likely evacuation destination and its phone number</li>
    </ul>
    <p>
      Behind it, two tiers. <strong>Essential:</strong> vaccination records including rabies, a medication list, prescription information, your veterinarian's contact details, the microchip number and registry company, and emergency contacts. These match the core of the <a href="https://www.cdc.gov/healthy-pets/emergency-preparedness/preparedness-kit.html" rel="noopener noreferrer">CDC's document list</a>.
    </p>
    <p>
      <strong>Important backup:</strong> a recent photo of your dog, ideally plus one of you together — <a href="https://www.ready.gov/pets" rel="noopener noreferrer">Ready.gov</a> recommends this because it documents ownership. Add proof of ownership or adoption, pet insurance details if applicable, and fuller feeding and care instructions.
    </p>
    <h3>Store it three ways</h3>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>Waterproof physical copies</strong> in the go-bag.</li>
      <li><strong>A secure digital copy</strong> in cloud storage or email.</li>
      <li><strong>Critical documents downloaded to your phone</strong>, so the rabies certificate opens when cell service is down.</li>
    </ul>
    <p>
      A fill-in emergency profile sheet makes the summary easy to keep current; a typed page you update twice a year works just as well.
    </p>

    <h2 id="food-water-medication">Food, water, and medication</h2>
    <p>
      The bag holds a few days of everything your dog consumes; the reserve beside it extends that toward the 7–14 days the ASPCA and CDC describe. A small portable kit and a larger reserve can coexist.
    </p>
    <h3>Food</h3>
    <p>
      Pack the food your dog already eats; an evacuation is a bad moment for an abrupt diet change. Pre-portion meals so you can feed without measuring, and keep everything sealed against moisture and pests — the CDC specifies waterproof containers. Include a manual can opener only if the food requires one.
    </p>
    <h3>Water</h3>
    <p>
      There is no reliable universal formula for emergency water, so do not pack to one. Measure what your dog drinks on an ordinary day and build from that, with margin for heat and exertion. Water is heavy; the bag may realistically hold a day or two, with the rest staged beside it. For carrying water on the move, our <a href={ROUTES.coolingHydration} data-track="collector_to_converter_click" data-from-page={pageSlug} data-to-page={ROUTES.coolingHydration} data-link-position="food-water-medication">dog travel hydration guide</a> compares bottles and bowls that work away from home.
    </p>
    <h3>Medication</h3>
    <p>
      Label every medication with your dog's name and keep prescription details and written instructions in the same pouch. <a href="https://www.ready.gov/pets" rel="noopener noreferrer">Ready.gov</a> recommends an extra supply of regular medication in a waterproof container. Whether you can keep one depends on the prescription, your veterinarian, pharmacy, insurer, and the medication itself, so ask your vet what is possible. Never change doses or stretch a schedule to build a reserve.
    </p>

    <h2 id="first-aid">First aid and emergency control</h2>
    <p>
      A compact pet first-aid kit is core gear. Its job is to help you respond while you get your dog to a veterinarian — not to replace one. The <a href="https://www.avma.org/resources-tools/pet-owners/emergencycare/first-aid-tips-pet-owners" rel="noopener noreferrer">AVMA's first aid guidance</a> suggests kit contents such as important phone numbers, a copy of your pet's medical record, a gauze roll, clean towels, saline solution for cleaning wounds or flushing eyes, and a digital thermometer. Disposable gloves and basic bandaging material round it out. The AVMA is also clear that any first aid should be followed by veterinary care.
    </p>
    <p>
      A kit that covers both people and dogs saves space.
    </p>

    <p>
      If your dog shows signs of serious injury, breathing distress, collapse, or heatstroke, contact a veterinarian or emergency animal hospital immediately.     </p>
    <h3>The emergency muzzle</h3>
    <p>
      A muzzle is limited, situational gear. An injured or frightened dog can bite even if normally gentle, most often while being handled. The AVMA advises applying a muzzle if a pet threatens to bite — and <strong>not muzzling a pet that is vomiting</strong>. Never use a muzzle in a way that interferes with breathing, and do not treat it as something every injured dog automatically needs.
    </p>

      Introduce a muzzle now, with treats, so it is routine rather than another frightening event.     </p>

    <h2 id="staged-gear">What stays beside the bag</h2>
    <p>
      The staged reserve is everything too large or heavy for the bag. Keep it in the same accessible spot so leaving is one trip: dog, bag, crate, reserve.
    </p>
    <h3>Travel crate or carrier</h3>
    <p>
      For a dog already comfortable in one, a crate is among the most useful things you can bring: containment in crowded places, simpler transport, temporary shelter, and an option at hotels and boarding facilities. A familiar enclosed space can also be the calmest spot in an unfamiliar room. The CDC recommends an appropriately sized carrier with bedding.
    </p>

    </p>

    <h2 id="comfort">Keeping your dog chill once you are out</h2>
    <p>
      Once the essentials are handled, a few familiar things make displacement easier. <a href="https://www.ready.gov/pets" rel="noopener noreferrer">Ready.gov</a> recommends favorite toys, treats, or bedding because familiar items can help reduce a pet's stress:
    </p>
    <ul>
      <li>The familiar travel bed or a blanket from home</li>
      <li>A favorite toy</li>
      <li>Treats and a long-lasting chew</li>
      <li>A lick mat for settling in a new space — see our <a href={ROUTES.calmingLickMats} data-track="collector_to_converter_click" data-from-page={pageSlug} data-to-page={ROUTES.calmingLickMats} data-link-position="comfort">lick mat comparison</a></li>
      <li>An anxiety wrap, if your dog already wears one</li>
    </ul>
    <p>
      The point is familiarity and routine — usual meal times, the same bed — not a pile of new calming products. None of these treat anxiety or guarantee calm behavior. If behavior changes persist after a disaster, the Red Cross advises talking to your veterinarian.
    </p>

    <h2 id="situational">Situational and regional add-ons</h2>
    <p>
      Extras depend on where you live: wildfire smoke and ash, extreme heat, freezing weather, flooding, debris, low visibility. Add only what fits your likely emergency:
    </p>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>Eye protection.</strong> Dog goggles such as Doggles shield the eyes; the <a href="https://www.akc.org/expert-advice/lifestyle/eye-protection-for-dogs/" rel="noopener noreferrer">AKC describes</a> them as protection from wind, dirt, debris, and other environmental hazards. They do nothing for your dog's lungs and do not make smoky air safe. They do make for a pretty good photo.</li>
      <li><strong>Paw protection.</strong> After a wildfire, <a href="http://publichealth.lacounty.gov/vet/docs/WildfirePetSafetyFactSheet.pdf" rel="noopener noreferrer">Los Angeles County Public Health</a> suggests training dogs to wear foot protection outdoors to guard against ash and debris, and wiping paws and coat with a damp cloth after walks.</li>
      <li><strong>Visibility gear.</strong> A reflective or LED collar or vest for evacuations at night or in poor visibility.</li>
      <li><strong>Cooling gear</strong> for heat, and a <strong>cold-weather layer</strong> for dogs with thin coats in freezing conditions.</li>
    </ul>
    <p>
      For wildfire smoke, the <a href="https://www.epa.gov/wildfires/protect-your-pets-wildfire-smoke" rel="noopener noreferrer">EPA's pet guidance</a> centers on limiting exposure: keep pets indoors as much as possible, keep bathroom breaks short, and limit activity. Short-nosed breeds and dogs with heart or lung disease are especially at risk. If your dog has trouble breathing, contact a veterinarian immediately. Any dog expected to wear goggles or boots needs gradual, treat-based practice first.
    </p>

    <h2 id="destination">Know where you are going</h2>
    <p>
      A go-bag does not solve the destination problem. <a href="https://www.ready.gov/pets" rel="noopener noreferrer">Ready.gov</a> warns that many public shelters and hotels do not allow pets inside, and the <a href="https://www.redcross.org/get-help/how-to-prepare-for-emergencies/pet-disaster-preparedness.html" rel="noopener noreferrer">Red Cross</a> says pets arriving at its shelters may need to be housed separately with the help of animal welfare groups. Keep a short list of:
    </p>
    <ul>
      <li>Pet-friendly hotels on your evacuation routes (the Red Cross suggests asking whether no-pet policies can be waived in an emergency)</li>
      <li>Local emergency shelters that accommodate pets</li>
      <li>Boarding facilities</li>
      <li>Emergency veterinary hospitals outside your immediate area</li>
      <li>Friends or family outside the likely evacuation zone</li>
    </ul>
    <p>
      Keep it in the document packet and on your phone. Ready.gov also suggests a buddy system with someone who can evacuate your dog if you cannot get home.
    </p>

    <h2 id="practice-and-maintenance">Practice before the emergency, then maintain the kit</h2>
    <p>
      Your dog should meet unfamiliar gear before the emergency: the crate or carrier, harness, muzzle, goggles or boots if you may use them, and the travel bed. Short, rewarded sessions are enough. Occasionally run the departure itself — leash, bag, crate, staged supplies, into the car. It is not a disaster drill; it is how you learn on a calm afternoon that the crate no longer fits the new car.
    </p>
    <h3>Review the kit every 6 months, and after any use</h3>
    <ul>
      <li>Confirm documents and vaccination records are current</li>
      <li>Verify microchip registry details and ID tags</li>
      <li>Update photos and medication information</li>
      <li>Recharge the power bank; check batteries</li>
      <li>Reassess quantities if diet, weight, medications, or health needs changed</li>
    </ul>
    <p>
      That does not mean consumables sit untouched for 6 months. Rotate food, water, medication, and medical supplies according to their storage requirements, expiration dates, veterinary instructions, and manufacturer guidance. The <a href="https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/general-pet-care/disaster-preparedness" rel="noopener noreferrer">ASPCA's disaster preparedness page</a> advises rotating stored pet food and water every 2 months. An easy habit: feed the go-bag food at home and repack fresh.
    </p>
    <div class="geo-callout">
      <p><strong>Common go-bag mistakes:</strong> packing so much the bag is hard to carry; expired food or first-aid supplies; outdated microchip or tag contact details; loose, unprotected paperwork; gear your dog has never worn; and storing the whole setup somewhere hard to reach.</p>
    </div>
