  <div class="prose">
    <p>
      Fleas and ticks are not niche hazards for wilderness dogs. They are routine outdoor-dog problems for a huge share of the United States, from mild coastal neighborhoods to humid southern yards to Northeast trail systems. A dog does not need a dramatic backcountry day to pick up fleas or come home with a tick.
    </p>
    <p>
      That is why this guide starts broader than the natural-only conversation. The real question is not whether one category sounds more appealing. It is what level of protection your dog actually needs based on region, season, household setup, and tolerance for risk.
    </p>

    <h2 id="exposure">How dogs get exposed in real life</h2>
    <p>
      Fleas usually arrive through infested animals, shared environments, boarding, wildlife traffic, or grass and soil where flea life stages are already present. They escalate fast. One flea can quickly become a home problem once eggs drop into bedding, rugs, furniture, and baseboards.
    </p>
    <p>
      Ticks are a different exposure pattern. They wait on grass, brush, wooded edges, and leaf litter, then grab a passing host. Dogs are lower to the ground than people, push deeper into vegetation, and spend more time with noses, ears, armpits, groins, and paws exactly where ticks like to attach.
    </p>
    <p>
      The basic prevention habits still matter even if you use medication: post-walk tick checks, flea combing when scratching starts to feel suspicious, washing bedding during heavy season, and avoiding the brushy edge where lawns turn into wild cover.
    </p>

    <h2 id="where-and-when">Geography and seasonality matter more than generic “summer flea season” talk</h2>
    <p>
      The Southeast deals with the highest year-round flea pressure and strong tick pressure too. The Northeast is the classic Lyme-disease conversation. The Pacific Coast is often underestimated because mild winters prevent the hard die-off owners expect. The Southwest adds a brown-dog-tick wrinkle because that species can thrive indoors in hot climates.
    </p>
    <p>
      Seasonality is just as uneven. Fleas stay active when temperatures remain warm enough, which means many owners effectively deal with spring-through-fall pressure and some deal with all-year pressure. Black-legged ticks can be active during winter warm spells, and the tiny nymph stage in late spring is one of the easiest to miss.
    </p>
    <p>
      The practical takeaway is simple: build the plan around your dog’s exposure and your region, not around a generic calendar date when you mentally switch from “winter dog” to “summer dog.”
    </p>

    <h2 id="medications">The conventional medication landscape</h2>
    <p>
      Conventional flea and tick control breaks into a few clear categories. Oral prescriptions are the easiest fit for owners who want no-mess dosing and strong tick kill. Bravecto stretches the dose interval to about 12 weeks. Monthly isoxazolines such as NexGard and Simparica are the most familiar format. Combo chews such as Simparica Trio and NexGard Plus add heartworm and intestinal-parasite coverage.
    </p>
    <p>
      OTC topicals are still relevant when owners want to avoid a prescription or reduce cost. Frontline Plus remains the familiar default. K9 Advantix II is the stronger caution-flag option because permethrin and cats do not mix casually. Seresto is the convenience play for owners who want an 8-month collar rather than a monthly routine.
    </p>
    <p>
      If you already know you want the strongest conventional options, skip to <a href={ROUTES.fleaTickMedications}>our medications comparison page</a>. That page is built for high-intent buyers. The goal here is helping you choose the category first.
    </p>

    <h2 id="rx-vs-otc">Prescription vs. OTC: the honest comparison</h2>
    <p>
      Prescription products tend to win on convenience, consistency, and tick-kill confidence. OTC products win on accessibility and lower friction at the moment you decide to act. Neither automatically wins on every dog.
    </p>

    <h2 id="natural-options">Natural alternatives have a place, but not an unlimited one</h2>
    <p>
      Natural flea and tick products are best treated as layered prevention tools. They appeal most to owners who want to reduce pesticide load, can maintain a more hands-on routine, and are dealing with lower or moderate pressure rather than a full home infestation.
    </p>
    <p>
      If that is already your preference, go to <a href={ROUTES.naturalFleaTickPrevention}>our natural flea and tick guide</a> for the deeper owner-to-owner read, or jump straight to <a href={ROUTES.naturalFleaTickProducts}>our natural product picks</a>. If you need bath-day help for cleanup rather than a prevention decision, use <a href={ROUTES.fleaSeasonBathTools}>our flea-season bath tools page</a>.
    </p>

    <h2 id="risks">Medication risks worth knowing before you buy</h2>
    <p>
      The big regulatory caveat on oral flea and tick prescriptions is the FDA neurologic warning for the isoxazoline class. That does not mean the class is condemned. The FDA still says these products are safe and effective for most dogs. It does mean seizure history is not a minor footnote.
    </p>
    <p>
      The big household caveat in OTC topicals is permethrin and cat toxicity. This is not a fussy internet detail. In mixed-pet homes, it should directly shape what you buy. Seresto needs a different kind of nuance: the adverse-event history and EPA review are legitimate context, but the product remains on the market and is still widely used.
    </p>

    <h2 id="see-your-vet">When a vet visit is worth it before you choose</h2>
    <p>
      If your dog has seizure history, kidney or liver disease, a complicated medication list, or very heavy tick exposure, a veterinarian visit is worth it before you guess. The same goes for owners in Lyme-heavy Northeast regions, RMSF-heavy southern regions, or homes where cats and dogs share beds, couches, and grooming contact.
    </p>
    <p>
      You do not need a dramatic emergency to justify a better prevention plan. You just need enough risk that guessing wrong would be more annoying, more expensive, or more dangerous than asking for tailored advice.
    </p>
