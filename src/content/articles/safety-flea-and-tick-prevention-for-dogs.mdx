  <div class="prose">
    <p>
      Fleas and ticks are not niche hazards for wilderness dogs. They are an issue for dogs across the United States, from mild coastal neighborhoods to humid southern yards to Northeastern trails. A dog does not need to go on a hike to pick up fleas or come home with a tick.
    </p>
    <p>
      The type of protection your dog needs is based on geographical region, time of year, household setup and tolerance for risk.
    </p>

    <h2 id="exposure">How Dogs Get Exposed</h2>
    <p>
      Dogs are often exposed to fleas by other dogs in shared environments and kennel boarding. They can also encounter them via wildlife or grass and soil. Fleas reproduce quickly and can quickly become a problem at home if eggs drop into bedding, rugs, furniture or baseboards.
    </p>
    <p>
      Ticks are a different exposure pattern. They wait on grass, brush, wooded edges, and leaf litter, then latch on to a passing host. Dogs are likely to get ticks because they are lower to the ground than people and push deeper into vegetation.
    </p>
    <p>
      Check your dog for fleas and ticks after walks, especially if they are scratching themselves more than usual. Wash your dog's bedding periodically to help prevent infestation.
    </p>

    <h2 id="where-and-when">Geography and Seasonality</h2>
    <p>
      In the Southeast, fleas and ticks are a year-round problem. Lyme disease is most prevalent in the Northeast. It is a bacterial infection transmitted primarily by the black-legged deer tick. On the Pacific Coast, mild winters prevent the hard die-off of fleas and ticks that occurs in other parts of the country. In the Southwest, the brown dog tick can thrive indoors in hot climates and is a primary vector for Rocky Mountain spotted fever.
    </p>
    <p>
      Seasonality also differs in various parts of the country. Fleas stay active when temperatures remain warm enough. For some, that is from spring until fall. For others, they are a year-round issue. Black-legged ticks can be active during winter warm spells, and the tiny nymph stage in late spring is one of the easiest to miss.
    </p>
    <p>
      Plan your dog's flea and tick prevention based upon your region and your dog's exposure.
    </p>

    <h2 id="medications">Pharmaceutical Medication</h2>
    <p>
      There are a few types of conventional flea and tick control. Oral prescriptions are best for owners who want no-mess dosing and strong tick kill. Bravecto stretches the dose interval to about 12 weeks. Monthly isoxazolines such as NexGard and Simparica are the most common. Combo chews such as Simparica Trio and NexGard Plus add heartworm and intestinal-parasite coverage.
    </p>
    <p>
      Over-the-counter topicals are best for owners who want to avoid a prescription medication or reduce cost. Frontline Plus is the most common. It is a monthly, waterproof topical treatment that kills fleas, ticks, and lice.
    </p>
    <p>
      K9 Advantix II is a topical treatment that kills and repels fleas, ticks, mosquitoes, biting flies, and lice. It is administered monthly. Note that this product is extremely toxic to cats.
    </p>
    <p>
      Seresto flea and tick collar is the most convenient, as it provides 8 months of continuous coverage.
    </p>
    <p>
      For more information, see <a href={ROUTES.fleaTickMedications}>our medications comparison page</a>.
    </p>

    <h2 id="rx-vs-otc">Prescription vs Over-the-Counter Medication</h2>
    <p>
      Prescription medications are effective for dogs with heavy flea and tick exposure, as they have a high parasite kill rate. They tend to be more expensive, because they require a veterinary visit. Note: Isoxazoline can cause neurological side effects.
    </p>
    <p>
      Over-the-counter medications are effective for most dogs with average flea and tick exposure. They do not require a veterinary visit, but they do require owners to read labels closely. Note: Permethrin is toxic to cats.
    </p>

    <h2 id="natural-options">Natural Alternatives</h2>
    <p>
      Natural flea and tick products are best treated as layered prevention tools. They appeal most to owners who want to reduce pesticide load, can maintain a more hands-on routine, and are dealing with lower or moderate flea and tick exposure.
    </p>
    <p>
      For more information, see <a href={ROUTES.naturalFleaTickPrevention}>our natural flea and tick guide</a> and <a href={ROUTES.naturalFleaTickProducts}>our natural product picks</a>.
    </p>
    <p>
      For flea bath recommendations, use <a href={ROUTES.fleaSeasonBathTools}>our flea-season bath tools page</a>.
    </p>

    <h2 id="see-your-vet">When to Seek Veterinary Care</h2>
    <p>
      If your dog has a history of seizures, kidney or liver disease, a complicated medication list, or very heavy tick exposure, consult your veterinarian. The same goes for owners in Lyme-heavy Northeast regions, Rocky Mountain spotted fever-heavy Southern regions, or homes where cats and dogs share beds and couches.
    </p>
