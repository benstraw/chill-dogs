    <p>
      AAHA cites a 30-60% increase in lost pets between July 4 and July 6, and shelters consistently see a spike right after the holiday. ASPCA also notes that many lost pets disappear after being frightened by loud noises, with fireworks high on that list. This is not a fringe problem. The encouraging part is that most of the useful protection is simple preparation done before the noise starts.

    <h2 id="one-week-before">One week before</h2>
    <p>
      The week before is when the preparation that actually matters happens. Last-minute interventions are less reliable because they ask your dog to accept new gear, new routines, or a vet plan at the same moment the stressor shows up.
    </p>
    <p>
      <strong>Verify ID and microchip.</strong> Rover survey data has found that many owners go six months or longer without checking whether their dog's ID tag is still accurate and readable. Check the tag now. Confirm the microchip registration is current and tied to a phone number you will answer, because a readable tag is still the fastest route home if your dog bolts.
    </p>
    <p>
      <strong>Test the anxiety wrap if you plan to use one.</strong> Pressure wraps work best when the dog already knows the feeling. If you are still comparing options, our guide to <a href={ROUTES.calmingAlternatives} data-track="collector_to_converter_click" data-from-page={pageSlug} data-to-page={ROUTES.calmingAlternatives} data-link-position="one-week-before">ThunderShirt alternatives</a> is for that planning window, not for the moment panic starts. ASPCA notes that an anxiety wrap may help in some cases, and a snug T-shirt can play a similar role if your dog already tolerates it.
    </p>
    <p>
      <strong>Start desensitization if you have time.</strong> Play recorded firework sounds at low volume during meals, calm play, or rest. Increase the volume gradually across short sessions. It is not a quick fix, but even a week of low-stress repetition can be more useful than doing nothing.
    </p>
    <p>
      <strong>Call your vet early if your dog has a history of panic.</strong> Prescription support for predictable noise events requires a veterinary conversation, and clinics get flooded with those requests right before the holiday. Calling a week out gives you time to discuss options and, if medication is prescribed, try a practice run before July 4. Never share medication between pets or adjust doses on your own.
    </p>
    <p>
      <strong>Test white noise or calm audio before the event.</strong> Get your dog used to a fan, sound machine, or steady TV audio in the room that will become the safe space. Familiar sound is part of the setup, not a same-night trick.
    </p>

    <h2 id="morning-of-july-4">Morning of July 4</h2>
    <p>
      <strong>Exercise early and on purpose.</strong> A long walk, secure fetch session, or other real activity in the morning can lower the restless energy your dog carries into the evening. The goal is not to exhaust your dog to the point of overstimulation. The goal is to make rest more available later.
    </p>
    <p>
      <strong>Feed earlier than usual.</strong> An anxious dog may stop eating once fireworks start. Feeding the main meal earlier means hunger is not piling on top of stress. If food enrichment usually helps, freeze a Kong or set up a lick mat during the day so it is ready later.
    </p>
    <p>
      <strong>Prep enrichment items your dog already knows.</strong> Stage a frozen Kong, lick mat, bully stick, or other long-duration chew that is already part of your dog's normal life. This is not the night to introduce novelty. Familiar items are much more likely to hold attention.
    </p>
    <p>
      <strong>Set up the calm room.</strong> Choose an interior room if you can, ideally one with fewer windows and less street noise. Put in bedding your dog already sleeps on, add the white noise source, and set up the crate only if your dog already uses it voluntarily. Leave the crate door open. Forced confinement during panic often makes things worse, not better.
    </p>

    </p>

    <h2 id="before-sunset">Before sunset</h2>
    <p>
      Fireworks in many neighborhoods start around dusk and continue unpredictably for hours. Do not wait for the first boom.
    </p>
    <p>
      <strong>Move the dog inside and close the perimeter.</strong> Close and latch doors, check gates, and secure dog doors before guests start moving in and out. AAHA specifically calls out gate and door security because holiday traffic creates easy escape routes.
    </p>
    <p>
      <strong>Close windows and blinds.</strong> Closed windows help with sound. Closed blinds reduce flashes and visual stimulation. Together they make the room feel more stable.
    </p>
    <p>
      <strong>Start white noise now.</strong> Turn it on before fireworks are audible. A fan, AC unit, TV, or white noise machine works best as background that is already present when the outside sound changes.
    </p>
    <p>
      <strong>Fit the wrap now if you are using one.</strong> Calm application is part of what makes it useful. Putting gear on a dog who is already reacting usually creates one more thing for them to fight.
    </p>
    <p>
      <strong>Take a final leash-only potty break.</strong> Even if you have a fenced yard, use the leash. A distant boom can be enough to send a dog over or through a fence they have respected a hundred times before.
    </p>

    <h2 id="during-fireworks">During fireworks</h2>
    <p>
      <strong>Stay calm yourself.</strong> Dogs read human behavior closely. Sitting nearby, watching TV, reading, or talking in a normal tone usually helps more than hovering and repeatedly checking on them.
    </p>
    <p>
      <strong>Offer reassurance if your dog wants it.</strong> The old idea that comfort reinforces fear is not a useful rule here. Fear is not a behavior your dog performs to get attention. If your dog seeks contact, give calm contact.
    </p>
    <p>
      <strong>Offer the enrichment item if your dog is still able to engage.</strong> A moderately stressed dog may work on a frozen Kong or lick mat. A dog in full panic may ignore food completely, and that tells you something important about the level of stress.
    </p>
    <p>
      <strong>Do not force crate confinement.</strong> If your dog does not already feel safe in the crate, forcing them inside adds a second problem. A bathroom, bedroom, or closet that your dog chooses voluntarily is usually better than a crate they are fighting.
    </p>
    <p>
      <strong>Do not take your dog to a fireworks display.</strong> Crowds, smell, flashes, and explosive sound at close range are flooding, not exposure therapy. If your dog is panicking hard enough to injure themselves, claw through barriers, or cannot recover, contact your veterinarian or an emergency animal hospital immediately.
    </p>

    <h2 id="after-fireworks">After fireworks</h2>
    <p>
      The noise stopping does not mean the risk is over.
    </p>
    <p>
      <strong>Sweep the yard before your dog goes outside.</strong> Fireworks debris can leave chemical residue and sharp fragments where dogs want to sniff and mouth. Walk the yard yourself first and throw away anything suspicious in a secured trash bin.
    </p>
    <p>
      <strong>Use leash potty breaks for the next day or two.</strong> Leftover neighborhood fireworks on July 5 are common. Even a usually reliable fenced yard can turn into a bolt risk when the dog is still carrying stress from the night before.
    </p>
    <p>
      <strong>Watch for lingering stress signs.</strong> Some dogs bounce back within hours. Others stay clingy, stop eating normally, withdraw, or startle more easily for a day or two. If that pattern hangs on or anything physical looks off, call your vet.
    </p>
